How to read this page

This dashboard has three kinds of information:

  • Observations are measured by a sensor in the last few minutes.
  • Official predictions are published by NOAA or the National Weather Service.
  • Predictions computed for this page computed here are calculated from federal data for this project. They are experimental and are not official NOAA or USGS products.

All federal data are public and provisional. Times on the dashboard are Eastern Time. Current is shown with flood (upriver) positive and ebb negative.

How USGS measures current

The USGS "current" on the dashboard uses the index-velocity method, the standard for tidal rivers:

1. A fixed acoustic Doppler meter on a pier or piling measures water speed in part of the channel every few minutes.
2. Boat surveys with a current profiler measure the whole cross-section over full tidal cycles. A rating relates the fixed meter's reading to the true mean velocity of the section.
3. Water level gives the area of the cross-section from a survey of the channel shape.
4. Discharge is the mean velocity times the area.

The dashboard shows the result of step 2 (USGS parameter 72255, "mean water velocity for discharge computation"). USGS reports downstream flow as positive; the dashboard reverses the sign so flood is positive.

What this means for marine navigation. It is the average over the whole section, bank to bank and surface to bed. It runs weaker than the current in mid-channel, and slack in the channel can come at a slightly different time. Treat it as a reliable sign of the direction and strength of the general current in the reach.

Method reference: Levesque and Oberg, 2012, Computing discharge using the index velocity method, USGS Techniques and Methods 3-A23.

Official predictions

NOAA tide predictions, Fort Pulaski (8670870)

NOAA's harmonic tide prediction in feet above MLLW. It is the astronomical tide only: no wind, pressure or river flow. NOAA's predictions are set to the 1983–2001 mean sea level, and sea level has risen since, so observed water in Savannah now runs about 0.6 ft above the prediction on an ordinary day.

NOAA tidal current predictions

NOAA measured currents with bottom-mounted profilers for a month or more at each point, analyzed the tide in the record, and publishes predictions from it. There is no meter at these points today. The predictions are for one depth at one spot in the channel and do not include river flow or wind. These are indicated by diamonds on the map.

National Weather Service wind forecast and alerts

Hourly forecast wind from the NWS Charleston office grid, one cell for the river and one for the entrance. Where the forecast gives a range, the dashboard uses the upper figure. Active NWS alerts are read for two points, one on the river and one at the entrance.

National Weather Service wave forecast (NWPS)

Forecast waves at the Grays Reef buoy and the Tybee approach come from the Nearshore Wave Prediction System, the wave model the NWS Charleston office runs for its coastal waters on a grid of about 1 nm. The dashboard reads significant wave height, peak period and the direction the waves come from at the grid cell nearest each place, hourly to four days ahead. New runs come about every six hours. At the buoy the forecast is drawn beside the observed wave height; at the Tybee approach only the forecast is provided, since there is no wave sensor.

Map

The chart layer is NOAA's electronic navigational chart display service. The street map is OpenStreetMap and the minimal gray map is Esri's light gray canvas. None is for navigation in this form.

Tide predictions at the USGS gauges

NOAA publishes no tide predictions at the USGS gauges, so they are computed here by harmonic analysis of each gauge's 15-minute record. They are the astronomical tide only and, like NOAA's, are set to the 1983–2001 mean level, so observed water runs about 0.6 ft above them.

Section-mean current predictions at the USGS velocity gauges

The same harmonic method applied to the USGS cross-section mean velocity. These let the dashboard show a current for future times at the three gauges that measure it. They do not include changes in river flow or weather.

At these gauges the dashboard shows this prediction, not NOAA's, beside the observed current, because it predicts the same thing the gauge measures. NOAA's nearest channel predictions are for one depth at a different spot and are listed as their own rows. From January to October 2026 the section-mean prediction differed from the observed current by 0.31 kt at Fort Pulaski and 0.27 kt at USACE Dock; NOAA's channel predictions differed by 0.55 and 0.42 kt, and at the Talmadge Bridge point the predicted current is about 40% weaker than the section mean at the dock.

Water level datums

Water levels are shown in feet above MLLW (mean lower low water, chart datum) for the 1983–2001 tidal epoch, so they can be compared with charted depths.

Fort Pulaski uses NOAA's published datum. The USGS gauges report gauge height on their own gauge datum (at or within half a foot of NAVD88), and USGS publishes no tidal datums for them, so the offset to MLLW at each was computed for this page computed here with NOAA's Tidal Analysis Datum Calculator (version 2.4.3), using Fort Pulaski as the control station. These are not official NOAA datums.

Channel forecast between gauges computed here

An experimental forecast of water level and current every 500 m along the ship channel, from the sea entrance to Port Wentworth, from 24 hours back to about 2.5 days after NOAA's model run. NOAA runs the model once a day (the 12:00 UTC run, posted mid-afternoon Eastern time), so the forecast reaches 1.5 to 2.5 days ahead depending on the time of day; the current forecast ends about six hours before the water level forecast. The correction from the live gauges is refreshed every hour, on the hour. At the five water level gauges and the three USGS velocity gauges, the dashboard uses this forecast for times ahead, in the cards, the caution and alert limits and the workable windows, and falls back to the tide-only prediction where the forecast ends or is more than 30 hours old.

Water level

Three parts are added together: (1) each gauge's tide, carried to places between gauges using the shape of the tide in NOAA's STOFS-3D-Atlantic model; (2) that model's weather signal (surge and set-down), smoothed and scaled; (3) a correction from how the live gauges have been running over the last few hours to three days, fading with time ahead.

Tested by predicting the Elba Island gauge from its neighbors only: the error was about 0.1 ft (4 cm) for the present and 0.3–0.45 ft (9–14 cm) one to three days ahead, against 0.4–0.65 ft for tide tables with the latest gauge offset held. The shaded bands on the charts are 95% limits.

Current

Current is predicted based on a 13 hour window related to water levels based on previous observations, plus a term for river flow at Clyo. Between observations it is blended from the two nearest places with observations. Above 30 km from the sea entrance, current is given only where observations exist for training.

The places with a current record are NOAA's 2023 survey meters (depth-mean current along the channel, about six weeks each) and the three USGS velocity gauges (section mean).

Known limits

Elba Island has no live current meter. The current there is NOAA's tidal prediction from the 2023 survey, or the experimental channel forecast.

USGS current is a section mean, not the current in the channel (see above).

Tide and tidal current predictions leave out weather and river flow. After heavy rain upstream or in strong winds, expect real conditions to differ.

The channel forecast is untested seaward of Fort Pulaski, where there is no water level gauge to compare to.

Transit distances are approximate, measured along the channel line.

All data are provisional and any feed can be late or down; the dashboard marks stale values and lists feeds that failed to load.
